What's at stake

How the midterms work and what the result changes in Washington.

A verdict on the president, two years in

The midterms fall halfway through a presidential term. Donald Trump is not on the ballot, but Congress is. Voters choose all 435 members of the House, 35 of the 100 senators and 36 governors. The new Congress takes office on Jan. 3, 2027, and sits until Trump's term ends in January 2029.

History weighs against the president. His party lost House seats in 18 of the 20 midterms since 1946. The only exceptions were 1998 and 2002.

Who votes

Any US citizen aged 18 or over who has registered. Each state runs its own election and sets its own rules on registration, mail voting and ID.

Turnout is lower than in a presidential year: about 50% of eligible voters in 2018 and 46% in 2022. The most motivated voters weigh more.

What changes with the result

  1. Laws

    Every bill must pass both chambers and get the president's signature. If Democrats win one chamber, Trump's legislative agenda stalls. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of both chambers.

  2. Budget

    Congress controls federal spending. A divided Congress makes budget deals harder and raises the risk of a government shutdown.

  3. Oversight

    The House majority runs investigations and issues subpoenas. A Democratic House could open inquiries into the administration. It can impeach with a simple majority, but removal needs two-thirds of the Senate.

  4. Nominations

    The Senate confirms cabinet members, ambassadors and federal judges by simple majority. A Democratic Senate could block Trump's picks, including any Supreme Court vacancy.

  5. Trade and tariffs

    Most tariffs rest on presidential powers. Congress can hold hearings and push for exemptions, but rolling tariffs back takes a law the president would likely veto.

  6. States and 2028

    36 governors are elected, most for four years. They lead on health, schools and policing, and will certify their state's results in the 2028 presidential race.

Whatever the result, the president keeps his own powers: executive orders, command of foreign policy and the veto.
